Moving Pima: a face, a voice and a map for a county’s first transportation plan.

Pima County’s first-ever Transportation Master Plan needed residents across 9,000 square miles to weigh in. I built the identity, the campaign and the graphics that carried it from Tucson festivals to rural pop-ups, and the information design that reported what happened.

The challenge

One plan, a huge county, and no single channel that reaches everyone.

The Transportation Master Plan sets long-range priorities for unincorporated Pima County, from Tucson’s edges to Ajo, Sells, Arivaca and Green Valley. As the county’s first, it had no existing name, look or audience, and it depended on residents telling the County where roads, bike lanes, sidewalks and transit fall short.

The engagement had to be transparent, multilingual and easy to join, and it had to repeat one consistent message across websites, ads, flyers, festivals, radio and email so that residents recognized it wherever they met it.

Everything pointed to one place: MovingPima.com, with an online survey and an interactive map where people could pin a specific concern.

01 · Logo creation

An M made of lanes.

The mark builds an M from overlapping, translucent bands, like travel lanes merging, in a palette that runs from county blue through desert greens to a warm citron. It had to sit comfortably beside the Pima County seal, survive a business-card QR layout and a sticker, and still read at email-signature size.

02 · Campaign assets

Your voice. Your routes. Your future.

The campaign put real people in motion — walkers, cyclists, wheelchair users, bus riders — on top of the route lines from the logo, so every piece showed who the plan was for. The same system stretched from report covers to the website, YouTube and Meta ads.

Cover directions

Three explorations for the plan’s report covers, all in County Blue: a crosswalk with a runner and a cyclist, a cropped rider with a bold diagonal wordmark, and a smiling cyclist crossing a ribbon of route color over Tucson landmarks.

03 · Public engagement support graphics

Every table, counter and inbox, one tap from the survey.

The outreach team needed things to hand out, post and forward. Every piece carries the mark, a single call to action and a QR code to MovingPima.com, with English and Spanish versions throughout, so a resident at a swap meet, an animal-care counter or a permit portal could join in seconds.

  • Partner toolkit. Ready-to-share English and Spanish graphics, a printable flyer and suggested newsletter copy, sent to about 150 community organizations.
  • Paper survey. 320 completed by residents without internet access or who wanted help.
  • Digital touchpoints. Banner and Meta ads, a permit-portal prompt and PCDOT email signatures, all pointing to the same URL.
04 · Information design

Showing where, when and how far the conversation reached.

The 18-page Public Input Report had to prove the outreach was broad and fair. I turned event logs, ad reports and coverage data into maps and charts the County could hand to its leadership and the public, each one answering a single question.

Results

More than 6,200 voices to shape 30 years of roads, paths and transit.

From February through July 2026, the consistent Moving Pima identity tied together festivals, rural pop-ups, ads, email, TV, radio and an influencer campaign. In-person outreach alone reached an estimated 1,000 to 1,200 residents, and input rose sharply after the July pushes.

The Public Input Report closed the loop, documenting every channel for county leadership and the public, and the input will guide the plan’s priorities.
